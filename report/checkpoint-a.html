Introduction

My aim is to grow wealth over many years and still be able to leave it alone when markets fall. I do not treat the money as one pile with one appetite for risk. I treat it as a managed portfolio with three blocks, and each block has one job.

Block Job What it holds Currency Size
Safety Pay ordinary life without forced sales Cash and short-term bonds Reais Twelve months of spending
Inflation Fund spending that already has a date NTN-B (Tesouro IPCA+), with maturities matched to those dates Reais Up to about 60 percent
Growth Grow wealth over the long run A fund of ETFs The currency of each ETF 25 to 40 percent

Safety. Twelve months of spending sit in cash and short-term bonds that can be reached in a few days. The return is not the point. This block turns a bad year in the market into a number on a screen, instead of a sale I am forced to make.

Inflation. Some future spending already has a date: retirement income, or a bill I can already name. A bond that pays a fixed amount can lose to rising prices. NTN-B pays inflation plus a real rate, in reais, and its maturity can match the date of the expense. The block is capped so that protecting the long run does not consume the portfolio.

Growth. This is money I can allow to fall, because no dated bill depends on it. It is equity, so it carries the most risk and the most expected return. It is also the block this course builds.

The first two blocks live in reais, the currency I spend. They keep spending and savings in the same currency. The growth block is different: it holds ETFs in their own currencies, and the mix of currencies is part of its diversification.

The growth block is a fund of ETFs

The growth block is an actively managed fund of ETFs. It is not tied to an index. A rule decides which ETFs it holds and what weight each one gets. The research question of this project is that rule: which ETFs earn a place, in what weight, so that the growth block compounds without breaking the first two blocks.

I do not pick companies. S&P Dow Jones Indices, in the SPIVA U.S. Scorecard for year-end 2024, found that about 84 percent of active large-cap funds trailed the S&P 500 over ten years, and more than 90 percent over fifteen. An ETF already gives a diversified basket at a low cost. The open decision is which ETFs, and in what weight.

Why this is worth studying

The three blocks are already chosen. The weights inside the growth block are not. The users are investors like me: people who spend in reais, keep a safety cushion and dated goals, and want the rest of their wealth to grow worldwide without picking stocks. I am the first. The application is the program that runs the rule for each of them.

The question is narrow. Who has studied a portfolio made of ETFs? And when someone held those ETFs in an optimized proportion, did the mix beat a simple alternative on data the rule had not seen? Trading rules such as trend following and technical analysis were outside this search, because the growth fund does not time markets.

The short answer

  • Holding ETFs instead of picking companies is well supported.
  • Optimizing the weights is not. An optimizer fed with past average returns rarely beats equal weight out of sample.
  • Portfolios of ETFs exist, but this three-block design does not.

Three warnings apply to any portfolio of ETFs:

  • Fees. A fund of funds can stack two layers of fees (Brown, Goetzmann and Liang 2002).
  • Behavior. Investors lost by timing trades and buying narrow ETFs. Broad, cheap ETFs hurt no one (Bhattacharya, Loos, Meyer and Hackethal 2017).
  • Wrapper. An Irish UCITS ETF usually pays 15 percent US withholding on dividends, against 30 percent for a US-listed ETF (Huang 2026).

This section says what the portfolio will do. It does not test it yet.

The managed portfolio

A program may change weights. It may not change the jobs.

  • Safety. Twelve months of spending in cash and short-term bonds, in reais. If the block is short, it is filled first.
  • Inflation. NTN-B matched to dated spending, capped at about 60 percent of the portfolio. Fixed ladder: about 20 percent short, 35 percent intermediate, 45 percent long.
  • Growth. Between 25 and 40 percent of the portfolio, in the growth fund. This is where the research happens.

The growth fund

What it holds. Every equity ETF the investor can buy: listed in the United States, as Irish UCITS, or on B3. Leveraged and inverse ETFs are excluded. The 61 ETFs in the research universe are only the first sample.

Exposure and wrapper. The same market is often sold in several ETFs, for example the S&P 500 as a US-listed, an Irish UCITS and a B3 ETF. That choice is the wrapper. The fund decides in two steps:

  1. Exposure: which market, and how much.
  2. Wrapper: which ETF delivers that market, by cost, domicile, dividend treatment and tax.

Currency. Each ETF is held in its own currency, unhedged, as part of the diversification. Results are measured in dollars, so the choice of ETFs is judged apart from the real. The conversion to reais is a separate line.

Limits. Caps per ETF, country and currency, set in Checkpoint B.

The screen an ETF must pass

Every candidate goes through the same tests:

  • a minimum history of monthly returns, needed for the backtest and the Monte Carlo simulation (proposed: five years);
  • return, volatility, and the depth of the worst fall;
  • correlation with the other candidates;
  • what is left after fees;
  • whether the result still holds on a period the rule did not see.

An ETF that fails gets no weight. A young ETF, such as AUVP11 or VWRA11, is recorded but not weighted.

The objective of the optimizer

The optimizer runs only inside the growth fund, and only on ETFs the screen approved.

  • Objective. The highest return per unit of risk, after fees.
  • Constraints. Long only and fully invested: no borrowing, no short sales. Caps per ETF, country and currency.
  • Inputs. Expected returns anchored to the world market portfolio, a shrunk covariance matrix, and minimum variance as a check.

Two benchmarks keep it honest:

  1. Equal weight on the same approved ETFs, on the same dates.
  2. One world ETF, held alone.

The optimizer wins only if its Sharpe ratio beats both benchmarks by at least 0.1, after fees, on data it has not seen. Otherwise the simple option wins, and that is the finding. The optimizer never takes money from the safety or inflation blocks.

How financial engineering does the work

The course's toolkit answers the two questions of the growth fund: which ETFs, and in what weight. Each step of the rule maps to a method.

Step Question Method Source
1. Describe Is this ETF worth a place? Return, volatility, worst fall, and Sharpe ratio after fees Sharpe (1994)
2. Look through Is it a new market or a copy? Correlation matrix and hierarchical clustering. A regression on market, size, value and momentum factors shows what the ETF really holds Sharpe (1963); Fama and French (1993); López de Prado (2016)
3. Weight How much of each ETF? Mean-variance optimization at the highest Sharpe ratio, long only, with caps Markowitz (1952)
4. Feed What returns should the optimizer expect? Equilibrium returns implied by the world market portfolio, and a shrunk covariance matrix Black and Litterman (1991); Ledoit and Wolf (2003); Grinold and Kahn (2000)
5. Cross-check Are the weights stable? Minimum variance and hierarchical risk parity, run beside the optimizer López de Prado (2016); Hudson and Thames (2020)
6. Test Would it have worked? Walk-forward backtest with costs, and a Monte Carlo bootstrap of monthly returns. A deflated Sharpe ratio corrects for the number of variants tried Bailey and López de Prado (2014)
7. Maintain How does it stay near the rule? Monthly bands, written as code, so the fund runs without discretion Assignment: automated, rule-based trading

Steps 1 and 2 choose the ETFs. Steps 3 to 6 set and test the weights. Step 7 keeps them.

Rebalancing

The same rule applies to the three blocks and to the ETFs inside the growth fund. Each target has a band a few percentage points wide.

  1. Once a month the program measures. Inside the band, it does nothing.
  2. Outside the band, new money goes first to whatever is most behind.
  3. Only if new money is not enough does it sell, and only back to the edge of the band, because a sale has a cost and a tax.

The promise is that the mix stays near the rule, not that rebalancing adds return.

What this is, in the language of the assignment

  • The fund. The growth fund: an actively managed fund of ETFs, inside a managed portfolio.
  • Method. Systematic portfolio selection and optimization, in the line of Markowitz and Sharpe.
  • Analysis. Rule-based, not fundamental stock picking. After an ETF is admitted, the program sees only returns, risk, fees and correlations.
  • Trading. Closer to buy-and-hold than to daily trading. Holdings change when a band breaks. The fund is long only. The rule aims for turnover below 20 percent a year in the growth fund. Checkpoint B measures the real figure.
  • Not used. Technical analysis and trend following are not part of the rule. The growth fund holds broad markets for the long run. The safety block, not a timed exit, absorbs a falling market. Trend following can be tested later as a variant.
  • Open question. A hedge with index options is not in the rule. One option chain I looked at had spreads too wide to serve as that hedge.
  • Cash. The cash share is the safety block, twelve months of spending.

Results

Checkpoint A asks for conceptual results, not a performance number. Nothing has been tested yet.

The main finding

An optimizer fed with past average returns rarely beats equal weight on data it has not seen. Expected returns are hard to estimate. Small errors in them become extreme weights, and those errors eat the gain from optimizing.

Optimizers do better when they lean on risk instead of return, use limits, and trade little. Even so, none wins every time. And no study I found tests a universe of ETFs against one world ETF, after fees.

So the most likely outcome of this project is a tie. That is still an answer: it would mean equal weight, or one world ETF, is enough. For the optimizer to count as the winner, it must beat both benchmarks by at least 0.1 in Sharpe ratio, after fees, on data it has not seen. Anything smaller is treated as noise.

What the reading changed in the design

  1. Anchored inputs. Expected returns come from the world market portfolio, not from each ETF's past average. The covariance matrix is shrunk. Minimum variance runs beside the optimizer as a check.
  2. Exposure before wrapper. In the first list of 61 ETFs, seven follow the S&P 500 and six follow a world index. The fund first chooses the market, then the cheapest and most tax-efficient ETF for it. Otherwise the optimizer would treat copies as diversification.
  3. Minimum history. Young ETFs, such as AUVP11 and VWRA11, are recorded but not weighted. Factor ETFs are judged on their live record, not their backtest.
  4. Fixed inflation block. No study supports moving NTN-B with the real yield. A fixed ladder keeps the focus on the growth fund.

Conclusions

  • The idea is new in its combination, not in its parts. Portfolios of ETFs, goal-based investing and inflation-linked bonds are all well studied. No published design I found combines them for an investor who spends in reais.
  • It makes rational sense. Two blocks protect spending in reais. The third block grows wealth worldwide, with the most risk and the most expected return.
  • It applies the course end to end. Factor regression and clustering choose the ETFs. Mean-variance optimization with equilibrium inputs sets the weights. Backtests, Monte Carlo and a deflated Sharpe ratio test them.
  • The gap is real. Almost no one has tested an optimized portfolio of ETFs against one world ETF, after fees. This project fills that gap.
  • The main risk is a tie. The literature suggests the optimizer may not beat equal weight or one world ETF by the 0.1 Sharpe margin. If it does not, the simple option wins, and that is a valid result.

1. Stock selection against the index

Claim. After costs, most actively managed equity funds trail a broad index, and the share that trails rises with the horizon. This holds in the US and in Brazil. The academic explanation combines costs (Fama and French), persistence that is explained by factors and expenses rather than skill (Carhart), and the concentration of long-run stock returns in a small number of firms (Bessembinder).

Sources and how they compare

  • Scorecards (practitioner, S&P Dow Jones Indices). The SPIVA PDFs returned HTTP 403, so figures come from secondary pages that quote them.
  • SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2024, as reported by ICFS: large-cap active funds vs. the S&P 500 underperformed at 65.2% (1 year), 76.3% (5 years) and 84.3% (10 years). The page says that above 90% underperform over 15 years without giving the exact figure.
  • SPIVA Latin America Mid-Year 2025, as reported by Funds Society (2025-10-29): over 10 years to 2025-06-30, 86.0% of Brazil equity funds trailed the S&P Brazil BMI; 66.3% of large-cap funds and 85.3% of mid/small-cap funds trailed their benchmarks.
  • A later SPIVA Latin America reading, as reported by B3 Bora Investir: 90.8% of Brazil equity funds trailed over 10 years. The page does not name the exact edition.
  • Academic.
  • Fama and French (2010): the aggregate US active fund portfolio is close to the market, and costs reach the investor as a lower return. Bootstrap tests find few funds whose returns cover their costs; before fees there is some evidence of skill in the extreme tails.
  • Carhart (1997): common factors and expenses explain almost all performance persistence; the persistence that remains sits in the worst funds. A replication (Choi and Zhao 2021, Critical Finance Review, listed on the FORRT page) finds no significant persistence in 1994 to 2018.
  • Bessembinder (2018), as reported by ASU: about 26,000 US stocks since 1926. 58% did not beat Treasury bills over their life, and 4.3% of firms generated the net wealth created. The journal page was blocked, and the ASU page has internal inconsistencies (86 vs. 90 firms; end year 2016 vs. 2017).

Agreement and disagreement. The sources agree that the average active fund loses after costs and that the losing share grows with the horizon. They disagree on skill in the tails: Fama and French find some before fees, while Carhart and its replication do not. In Brazil, large-cap funds do better than mid/small-cap funds over 10 years, though most still lose.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It justifies building the growth sleeve from index ETFs rather than from stock pickers. It does not show that an optimizer choosing weights among ETFs will beat a broad index: that is a different decision, and Theme 2 covers it.

2. Portfolio optimization against simple diversification

Claim. Mean-variance optimization that uses sample means usually loses to equal weight (1/N) out of sample, because errors in the estimated expected returns dominate. Optimizers that estimate the mean conservatively, rely mostly on the covariance (shrinkage or constraints), or trade little can beat 1/N. No method beats 1/N robustly and simply across datasets.

Sources and how they compare

  • Markowitz (1952) is the theoretical basis; it contains no out-of-sample test.
  • DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009): none of 14 models, tested on 7 datasets, consistently beats 1/N in Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return, or turnover. The estimation window needed to beat 1/N would be about 3,000 months with 25 assets and about 6,000 months with 50 assets.
  • Kritzman, Page and Turkington (2010) challenge this. Across 13 datasets, 1,028 series and more than 50,000 optimized portfolios, optimizers beat 1/N out of sample when they use long samples, simple expected-return assumptions, or minimum variance. Optimizing the covariance alone already adds value.
  • Kirby and Ostdiek (2012) attribute the DGU result to the test design (high estimation risk and extreme turnover). Low-turnover volatility-timing and reward-to-risk-timing strategies beat 1/N even with high transaction costs.
  • Jagannathan and Ma (2003): a no-short-sales constraint is equivalent to shrinking the covariance matrix. Constrained minimum-variance portfolios perform about as well as factor-model and shrinkage portfolios.
  • Ledoit and Wolf (2003 working paper): shrinking the sample covariance lowers tracking error and raises the realized information ratio. The 2004 journal version was not verified on a page.
  • Becker, Gürtler and Hibbeln (2015) test Michaud resampling: Markowitz beats Michaud on average, and the choice of estimator and constraints matters more than the optimization method. No primary Michaud source was opened.

Agreement and disagreement. All agree that estimating expected returns is the central problem. DGU conclude that 1/N is hard to beat in practice. KPT and Kirby and Ostdiek show it can be beaten with disciplined estimates and low turnover. Jagannathan and Ma and Ledoit and Wolf locate the useful part of optimization in the covariance. None of these studies tests a universe made only of ETFs against one broad world ETF.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It justifies treating equal weight as a serious benchmark rather than a straw man. It also supports long-only constraints, which act as shrinkage. A maximum-Sharpe objective depends on expected-return estimates, which is the input DGU show to be the weakest. The literature does not establish that the proposed optimizer will beat equal weight or a single world ETF out of sample; it suggests that minimum-variance or covariance-only variants have better odds.

3. Factor ETFs

Claim. Published factor premia shrink after publication and after a product launches. Many factors fail stricter statistical standards, costs remove the high-turnover anomalies, and the long side captures only part of value and momentum. On average, smart-beta ETFs deliver roughly the market return or less after fees once they are live.

In-sample evidence (and its reliability)

  • Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016): a new factor should clear a t-statistic of about 3.0, and most claimed findings are likely false.
  • Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020): of 452 anomalies, 65% fail at |t| > 1.96 and 82% at |t| > 2.78; 96% of trading-friction anomalies fail. Those that replicate are much smaller than originally reported.

After costs and long-only

  • Novy-Marx and Velikov (2016): most anomalies with turnover below 50% a month keep a significant net spread, while few high-turnover strategies survive. Size, value and profitability have the largest capacity.
  • Israel and Moskowitz (2013): the long side accounts for almost all of the size premium, 60% of the value premium and about half of momentum. Value is weak among the largest stocks.

Out of sample and after launch

  • McLean and Pontiff (2016): the bibliographic page was opened, but it carries no abstract. The commonly quoted declines (26% lower out of sample, 58% lower after publication, across 97 predictors) are not verified on a page and should be checked before citing.
  • Huang, Song and Xiang (2020, SSRN), as reported by InvestmentNews: smart-beta indices beat the market by 2.77% a year before ETF listing (backtest) and lagged it by 0.44% a year after listing, net of fees. The authors attribute the gap to data mining.

Agreement and disagreement. The sources converge that backtests and published premia overstate future returns. They differ on how much survives: Novy-Marx and Velikov and Israel and Moskowitz suggest that low-turnover tilts (value, profitability, size) keep part of the premium, while Huang et al. find no average excess return for ETFs after listing. No verified study isolates quality ETFs or dividend ETFs specifically.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It justifies discounting any factor ETF's backtest. Only post-launch track records should feed the optimizer's expected returns, and low-turnover tilts are preferable. It does not justify assuming that a quality, dividend or value tilt will add return after fees.

4. Inflation-linked government bonds as the long-horizon safe asset

Claim. For a long-horizon, risk-averse investor, the riskless asset is not cash. It is an inflation-indexed bond whose maturity matches the horizon or liability, because it hedges changes in real interest rates. As risk aversion rises, the myopic demand for risky assets goes to zero but the intertemporal hedging demand remains. How much weight goes to risky assets depends on their expected premium over the real rate, so a higher real yield makes the indexed bond relatively more attractive.

Sources and how they compare

  • Campbell and Viceira (2001): conservative investors should hedge falling real rates, and long inflation-indexed bonds are the right instrument. Nominal bonds are a substitute only when inflation risk is low. Their 2002 book (Strategic Asset Allocation, OUP) develops this in a chapter of the same name, but its page was seen only in search results, so it is not cited.
  • Brennan and Xia (2002) solve the finite-horizon problem with only nominal assets. Hedging demands depend on horizon, risk aversion and bond maturity, and the bond maturity should be chosen to match the horizon.
  • Kothari and Shanken (2004): simulated indexed-bond returns have lower real volatility and much lower correlation with stocks. Efficient portfolios hold substantial indexed-bond weights, which TIPS data from February 1997 to July 2003 broadly confirm.
  • Campbell, Shiller and Viceira (2009): long real yields fell from the 1990s to 2008, then spiked in the crisis, and breakevens collapsed. Even so, indexed bonds still provide a safe asset for long-term investors, despite short-run volatility and illiquidity.

Agreement and disagreement. The sources agree that a maturity-matched indexed bond is the lowest-risk asset for a long-horizon investor. Brennan and Xia is the nominal-only case and converges with Campbell and Viceira once indexed bonds exist. The 2009 paper adds the main caveat: mark-to-market volatility and liquidity risk in stress. No verified source gives a formula linking the optimal weight to the level of the real yield; that link is a reading of the models, not a tested result.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It justifies an inflation-linked, maturity-matched block to fund a dated liability in the spending currency (in Brazil, NTN-B). It does not justify a specific weight, and it does not settle how high the cap should be. No verified academic study models optimal NTN-B allocation or Brazilian sovereign risk.

5. Currency for an investor who spends in an emerging-market currency

Claim. The hedge decision depends on how each currency co-moves with the risky portfolio, measured in the currency the investor consumes in (economic exposure), not the currency of the account. For foreign bonds, the evidence favors hedging close to fully. For foreign equities, the answer depends on the home currency: holding "safe-haven" currencies (USD, EUR, CHF) unhedged lowers equity portfolio risk.

Sources and how they compare

  • Perold and Schulman (1988): hedging has a long-run expected return near zero, so it is a "free lunch" that cuts risk. They recommend a hedged benchmark and treating currency exposure as an active decision.
  • Black (1989): in an idealized world, hedge foreign equities at the same ratio for every country, always below 100%. The ratio does not depend on where the investor lives.
  • Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros and Viceira (2010): in 1975 to 2005, USD, EUR and CHF moved against global equities. A risk-minimizing equity investor should hold them even though their average return is low. For global bonds, minimum risk is close to a full hedge with a modest long USD position.
  • Schmittmann (2010, IMF): for German, Japanese, UK and US investors in 1975 to 2009, hedging cuts volatility substantially at quarterly horizons, and the benefit persists up to five years, contrary to earlier studies.
  • Vanguard (2026), "The FX dimension": the rule of thumb is to hedge bond exposures and leave most foreign equities unhedged. Only the summary page was opened.

Agreement and disagreement. All five agree on hedging foreign bonds. On equities they disagree: - Perold and Schulman favor a full hedge. - Black favors a partial, universal hedge. - Campbell et al. contradict "universal" by showing the answer depends on the home currency. - Vanguard lands in between, leaving most equities unhedged.

US vs. Brazilian investor. These are not the same case. For a US investor, the home currency is itself a safe haven, so leaving foreign equities unhedged adds exposure to procyclical currencies. For an investor spending in BRL, a risk-on currency, unhedged USD assets tend to gain when global equities fall. This is an inference from Campbell et al.'s framework; no verified study tests BRL directly.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It supports hedging foreign bonds and supports leaving foreign equities largely unhedged for a BRL spender. It also implies that the safety and inflation blocks, which fund BRL spending, belong in BRL instruments. It does not quantify the hedge ratio for BRL.

6. Rebalancing

Claim. Rebalancing exists to keep risk near the target allocation, not to add return. No frequency or band is optimal for risk-adjusted return, and infrequent monitoring with tolerance bands cuts trading. Whether a "rebalancing bonus" exists is disputed.

Sources and how they compare

  • Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering (2010, Vanguard; a third-party copy of the PDF was opened). They state that the main goal is to minimize risk relative to the target, not to maximize returns. Risk-adjusted returns are similar whether rebalancing is monthly, quarterly or annual. Trading differs widely: 1,008 rebalancing events for monthly with no band vs. 15 for annual with a 10% band. They suggest monitoring once or twice a year with roughly 5% bands. The section on using new cash flows first was not verified on the pages read.
  • Daryanani (2008), executive summary only: look often, trade rarely. Reviewing every two weeks and rebalancing only the asset classes outside wide bands "more than doubled" the benefit vs. annual rebalancing.
  • Willenbrock (2011): the diversification return comes from rebalancing itself (selling winners, buying losers), not from lower variance. It explains most of the excess return of the Gorton and Rouwenhorst commodity index.

Agreement and disagreement. All agree that bands are cheaper than frequent calendar rebalancing. They disagree on return: Vanguard sees no reliable gain, Willenbrock shows a real effect among volatile, weakly correlated assets, and Daryanani claims a gain. A replication reported to find Daryanani's gain statistically insignificant was not verified. Vanguard's 2022 "Rational rebalancing" paper could not be opened (broken links).

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It justifies band-based checks on a set calendar, framed as risk control around the mandate. It also makes routing new cash to underweight sleeves before selling a reasonable cost saving, though the primary source for that point was not verified. It does not justify promising a return from rebalancing.

7. Funds of ETFs and ETF-only portfolios

Claim. Goal-based designs split wealth into sub-portfolios, each with its own risk limit. Das et al. show this can be reconciled with mean-variance efficiency. Actively managed ETFs that hold only other ETFs exist in the market, and fee layering is the main structural risk. No verified product combines a 12-month cash floor, a capped inflation-linked sleeve, and an optimizer-weighted ETF growth sleeve.

Sources and how they compare

  • Das, Markowitz, Scheid and Statman (2010): each mental account has a return threshold, and its risk is the probability of missing it. Mean-variance, mental-account and VaR formulations are mathematically equivalent, and the aggregate portfolio is efficient when short sales are allowed. Banning short sales costs little in certainty-equivalent terms.
  • Brown, Goetzmann and Liang (2002): in funds of hedge funds, layered fees can, under some conditions, exceed realized returns. The authors propose an alternative fee structure.
  • BlackRock (2026), iShares LifePath Target Date 2035 ETF fact sheet: an active ETF launched 2023-10-17, with 13 holdings, all iShares ETFs. Total expense is 0.10%, made up only of acquired fund fees; the management fee is 0.00%. As of 2026-06-30 it held 66.10% equity, 33.45% fixed income and 0.44% cash.

Nearest neighbor. The iShares LifePath ETF is the closest verified structural analog: an actively managed ETF holding only ETFs, with no second fee layer. The match is only moderate: - Weights follow a target-date glide path, not a maximum-Sharpe optimizer. - There is no explicit 12-month cash floor (0.44% cash). - No TIPS or T-bill ETF appears among the top holdings, so there is no capped inflation sleeve. - It is built for a US investor, not one spending in BRL.

Das et al. is the closest academic analog for separating the three blocks.

Agreement and disagreement. Das et al. formalize goal-based bucketing and show it can be reconciled with whole-portfolio optimization. Brown et al. document harm from fee layering. The LifePath structure (acquired fund fees only, zero management fee) shows one way to avoid a second fee layer.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It supports separating the safety, inflation and growth blocks with distinct risk limits, and it supports a fee design that avoids double layering. It does not provide evidence on how such a fund performs, because no verified close match exists.

8. Portfolios of ETFs in depth (second research pass)

Claim. Direct evidence on optimizing weights across ETFs is thin. What exists points one way: the value of an ETF portfolio comes from broad, cheap funds, low turnover, and not believing backtests. Sophisticated weighting (maximum Sharpe, HRP) has no verified, peer-reviewed, out-of-sample win over equal weight in an ETF universe after costs.

8.1 Optimizing across ETF universes. - No peer-reviewed study that optimizes an ETF universe against equal weight with costs could be opened. - López de Prado (2016, Journal of Portfolio Management), "Building Diversified Portfolios that Outperform Out-of-Sample", introduces hierarchical risk parity (HRP). The SSRN page was blocked and the mirrored PDF could not be read. Two points come only from search snippets and are not verified: - the test uses Monte Carlo simulation, not real ETFs; - HRP has lower out-of-sample variance than Markowitz. - The paper does not show a return advantage, and it is not a study of ETFs. - Kipnis (2017) replicates the HRP weights in R and places HRP between unstable mean-variance and inverse volatility, but runs no comparative backtest. - Two ETF-universe studies were seen only in search results and are not cited: a 2025 Central European University thesis on 30 US ETFs, and Kaucic and Valentinuz (2018) on 56 ETFs listed in Italy.

8.2 How investors use ETFs. - Bhattacharya, Loos, Meyer and Hackethal (2017, Review of Finance, not Review of Financial Studies) study 6,949 clients of a German broker in 2005 to 2010, of whom 1,080 traded ETFs. - Adopting ETFs did not improve portfolios, even before trading costs. - The HKUST summary attributes the loss mainly to poor timing and to choosing narrow sector and country ETFs. No investor group lost by holding cheap, broadly diversified ETFs.

8.3 ETF strategists and managed ETF portfolios. - In 2014 the SEC settled with F-Squared (AlphaSector) for USD 35 million, as reported by Financial Advisor. - The 2001 to 2008 track record was a backtest of an algorithm that only existed from September 2008. A signal-timing error inflated the hypothetical results by about 350%. - This is the clearest practitioner warning against marketing an optimized ETF strategy on in-sample results. - Morningstar's ETF managed portfolio landscape reports were seen only in snippets and are not cited.

8.4 Robo-advisors. - D'Acunto, Prabhala and Rossi (2019, Review of Financial Studies; opened as CESifo WP 6907): - investors who held fewer than 5 stocks before adopting the robo-advisor gained diversification, lower volatility and better performance; - investors who held more than 10 stocks gained little and traded more. - The optimizer studied allocates across stocks, not ETFs. - Betterment's public methodology (2026), a primary practitioner source: - runs mean-variance on equilibrium returns obtained by reverse optimization from the global market portfolio, blended with forecasts from large asset managers; - averages maximum-Sharpe weights over thousands of simulated 15-year samples (a form of resampling); - caps deviations from an ACWI IMI benchmark. - Betterment's design amounts to optimization anchored on the broad world portfolio rather than on historical means.

8.5 The Brazilian investor: vehicles and tax. - State Street (Huang, 2026): an Irish UCITS ETF generally suffers 15% US dividend withholding at fund level under the US-Ireland treaty. A US-listed ETF is often withheld at 30% at the investor level when no treaty applies. UCITS ETFs trade with lower on-screen liquidity and wider spreads. The article does not address US estate tax. - B3 Bora Investir (updated 2024-01-10) describes the rules for B3-listed equity ETFs and BDRs of ETFs: - capital gains are taxed at 15% (20% for day trades), assessed monthly, with no exemption threshold; - dividends from BDRs of US ETFs bear 30% US withholding, IOF and depositary fees, and go to the Carnê-Leão with a credit for the US tax. - The B3 page predates the effects of Law 14.754/2023 on individuals. The law's text could not be opened, so the 15% annual regime for offshore holdings is not verified here. - ETFs Brasil comparison page: the B3-listed world fund VWRA11 (launched 2025-09-30) charges 0.52% a year and holds the Vanguard FTSE All-World UCITS ETF. The S&P 500 feeder IVVB11 charges 0.23%. A single world fund is therefore available on B3, but at more than twice the fee of the S&P 500 feeder.

Agreement and disagreement. The sources agree that unconstrained optimizers are unstable and that investor behavior (timing, narrow funds) destroys more value than weighting choices add. They differ in remedy: López de Prado proposes HRP, while Betterment anchors mean-variance on market weights and resamples. Neither offers verified evidence of beating equal weight or one world ETF out of sample after costs.

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. It supports a growth sleeve of broad, low-cost ETFs, an optimizer anchored on the world portfolio with tight bounds, and a strict ban on presenting backtests as track records. For a BRL investor it makes the choice of vehicle (UCITS, US-listed, B3 feeder) a first-order fee and tax decision. Whether the optimizer beats equal weight remains unproven.

9. Practitioner and non-academic views on portfolio optimization

Claim. The practitioner sources that could be verified share the academic diagnosis: expected returns are the weak input, not the optimizer. They answer with risk-based methods, market-anchored inputs, constraints, and careful rebalancing. None shows an optimizer beating a simple market-anchored portfolio.

Sources and how they compare - Open-source tools. - PyPortfolioOpt documentation (Robert Martin) warns that poor expected-return estimates can do more harm than good. It advises investing effort in the risk model and offers Black-Litterman. - Riskfolio-Lib documentation (Dany Cajas) supports HRP, HERC, NCO, risk parity and Black-Litterman. Its warnings are mostly about solvers. - Portfolio Visualizer. Its optimizer page states that the result does not predict which allocation will do best outside the chosen period. In practice it is an in-sample tool. - Risk parity. Bridgewater's "The All Weather Story" (2012) balances risk across four growth and inflation regimes so that no forecast of the regime is needed. It is the practitioner root of risk parity. - Rebalancing timing. - Newfound Research (Hoffstein, 2013): changing only the day of the month of rebalancing moved the maximum drawdown of a 10-month moving-average rule on SPY from 19.03% to 30.22%. Spreading the rebalance over the month removed this dependence with little extra turnover. - Newfound (Hoffstein, 2019): semiannually rebalanced smart-beta portfolios showed 100 to 400 bps of dispersion from rebalance date alone. - Equal weight. Rational Reminder episode 394 (Felix, Bortolotti, Wilson, 2026-01-29) concerns 1/N across stocks, not across asset-class ETFs: - equal-weight S&P 500 funds beat cap weight in backtests since 1971 but trailed in live funds over the last 10 years; - the hosts attribute the gap to size and value tilts with negative momentum; - turnover is about 10 times that of cap weight. - Asset-allocation comparisons. Meb Faber's Global Asset Allocation (2015) compares 60/40, risk parity, the Permanent Portfolio, the global market portfolio and endowment-style allocations. Only the launch page was opened; the book's conclusion is not verified. - YouTube. Titles and channels were confirmed through YouTube's oEmbed endpoint, but descriptions and transcripts were not accessible. Their content is reviewed in the lectures and talks annex: - two explainers from the Finance Research Gate channel, on HRP and on risk parity; - a Lead-Lag Media episode on risk parity; - a Cornell Financial Engineering Manhattan seminar by López de Prado on why machine-learning funds fail; - from Brazil: BTG Pactual's "Investindo pelo Mundo" on UCITS ETFs, and Abner Araujo comparing IVV and VT.

Agreement and disagreement. Practitioners agree with DeMiguel et al. (2009) that estimation error dominates and that in-sample optima do not transfer. They split on the remedy: - tool builders promote HRP and risk parity, without verified out-of-sample wins over 1/N; - Betterment anchors on the market portfolio and resamples; - Rational Reminder treats equal weight among stocks as a costly factor bet.

No verified practitioner source engages with Kritzman et al. (2010).

What this justifies for the three-block portfolio. Five practices follow: - replace historical means with equilibrium or anchored inputs; - bound weights and turnover; - report the optimizer's results as descriptive, not predictive; - spread rebalancing over time or use bands to reduce timing luck; - keep 1/N across ETFs distinct from 1/N across stocks.

It does not justify claiming that any of these methods will outperform.

1. Out-of-Sample Optimization

The Claim

Out-of-sample portfolio optimization is severely degraded by covariance matrix estimation error and matrix inversion (Σ⁻¹), which amplifies sample noise and creates radical weight instability across estimation windows. While naive equal weighting (1/N) eliminates parameter estimation error, it introduces high turnover, unintended sector bets, and negative momentum drag. Advanced structural techniques—such as Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP), Equal Risk Contribution (ERC / Risk Parity), and Marchenko-Pastur covariance denoising—achieve superior out-of-sample stability and higher risk-adjusted returns without incurring the costs and momentum penalties of equal weighting.

2. Marcos López de Prado Talks

The Claim

Quantitative investment strategies frequently fail out-of-sample because conventional finance relies on flawed methodological practices: forced integer differentiation (d = 1), time-based sampling, arbitrary labeling, and uncorrected backtesting. Achieving sustainable out-of-sample alpha requires adopting machine learning architectures grounded in financial theory, including fractional differentiation to preserve memory, non-time sampling (dollar/volume bars), meta-labeling for bet sizing, causal factor selection via Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs), and strict backtest overfitting controls like the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR).

Verified Video Evidence

Cornell Financial Engineering Manhattan (CFEM) — Marcos López de Prado, "The 7 Reasons Most Machine Learning Funds Fail" (2019-05-13) https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=npA8sI-k328:

  • Reason 1 (Sisyphus Paradigm): Siloed researchers working independently produce false discoveries; firms should operate collaborative "meta-strategy assembly lines."
  • Reason 2 (Over-differentiation): Computing integer returns (d = 1) erases historical memory/signal. Fractional differentiation (d ∈ (0,1)) achieves stationarity while preserving memory.
  • Reason 3 (Canonical Sampling): Time-based bars sample arbitrarily; dollar and volume bars synchronize sampling with information arrival.
  • Reason 4 (Wrong Labeling): Standard fixed-horizon labeling ignores path risk. The triple barrier method and meta-labeling (secondary model deciding bet execution) optimize trade size and precision.
  • Reason 5 (Non-i.i.d. Samples): Overlapping financial outcomes violate i.i.d. assumptions; sample uniqueness weights must be applied.
  • Reason 6 (Cross-Validation Failure): Standard k-fold CV leaks future information; purged and embargoed cross-validation must be used.
  • Reason 7 (Backtest Overfitting): Repeated backtesting generates inflated Sharpe ratios. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) adjusts for N_trials, sample length, skewness, and kurtosis.

Reitan Kapital — Prof. Marcos López de Prado, "Professor Marcos Lopez de Prado on Machine Learning in Asset Management - Kapitaleierdagen 2024" (2024-12-17) https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fOCzNFwmM7Y:
